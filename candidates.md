# Candidates — Ridiculous at First Glance, Real by the Second

Competition: `u-po-pu-ridiculous-at-first-glance-real-by-the-second-9a97d12a`
Judge brief criteria, in order: (1) bizarre startup pitch that makes business sense;
(2) genuinely bizarre — not a familiar business with a strange adjective;
(3) business logic holds — clear why someone pays; (4) lands as a pitch —
one sentence, ≤200 chars, laugh-to-nod turn; (5) concrete and vivid —
names the thing sold and who buys it.

Target: 100–160 chars, at least two beats (setup → turn), one concrete
detail, punch in the last 3–5 words. All 12 below were counted with Python
`len()` and sit inside 100–160 chars.

## The 12 candidates

1. (142) We hire professional mourners for weddings, so the groom's side looks loved — because nobody books a venue for a family that can't fill a pew.
2. (138) We write your dog's autobiography and publish it on Amazon, because owners will pay anything to see their rescue mutt become a bestseller.
3. (121) We train pigeons to deliver breakup letters across the city, because a bird at your window hurts less than a text at 2am.
4. (138) We bury your dead smartphone in a tiny coffin and mail you the funeral photos, because people grieve cracked screens harder than goldfish.
5. (136) We rent a professional apologiser to say sorry to your in-laws for you, because one sincere stranger costs less than a ruined Christmas.
6. (130) We sell insurance against bad wedding photos, paying out in a free reshoot if your mother-in-law blinks in more than half of them.
7. (124) We mail your houseplant a handwritten apology every time you forget to water it, because guilty owners pay to feel forgiven.
8. (140) We sell pre-written breakup texts read aloud by a retired Shakespearean actor over the phone, because dumping someone hurts less as theatre.
9. (142) A gym that fines you every time you skip leg day and donates the fine to your rival gym, because shame is the only trainer that never cancels.
10. (123) We charge offices to pipe fake keyboard clatter into video calls, because managers pay good money for productivity theatre.
11. (138) We rent out a professional queue-stander who holds your place at product launches, then auctions your spot when the line reaches the door.
12. (125) We sell bottled air from your childhood hometown, because homesick expats will pay $40 to breathe the street they grew up on.

## Manual ranking (criteria applied in the brief's order — no judge API used)

**Criterion 2 cuts first.** C9 (gym fines) is a real business — GymPact/StickK
already fine people for skipping — with a strange adjective bolted on, exactly
what the brief says loses. C11 (queue-stander) is a known TaskRabbit-style
service. C10 (fake keyboard clatter) and C12 (bottled hometown air) are known
gags sold for years. All four fall here, however funny they read.

**Criterion 3 among the survivors.** C2 has the strongest payer logic: pet
owners demonstrably spend billions, and a real published book on Amazon is a
tangible product — "rescue mutt become a bestseller" is a believable emotional
purchase. C6 is close (weddings are $30k+ spends, photo anxiety is real,
insurance + free reshoot is a coherent product). C1 and C5 are strong too
(wedding social pressure; in-law conflict with a clean cost comparison).
C3 is weaker: pigeons cannot reliably deliver letters, so the "real business"
leg wobbles. C4 is weakest here: the funniest premise, but nobody actually
pays for a phone funeral — the payer logic is a joke, not a market. C7's
payer (guilty plant owners buying apology letters) is thinner still, and C8's
"read aloud over the phone" is a thinner product than C3's pigeons.

**Criterion 4 (turn) and 5 (concrete).** C2 closes on "become a bestseller"
— the punch sits in the last three words — and names Amazon and the rescue
mutt. C1 closes on "can't fill a pew" and C5 on "a ruined Christmas", both
strong, but C1's core (rent-a-mourner) is a real service in the UK, so a
knowledgeable judge may recognise it and dock criterion 2; C5's apologiser
is closer to a familiar mediator than a dog's autobiography is to anything.
C6's funniest detail (mother-in-law blinks) sits mid-sentence, so its punch
lands softer than C2's.

## Pairwise checks against strong rival shapes

- C2 vs a generic pet pitch ("dog birthday party planner"): C2 wins — an
  autobiography published on Amazon is stranger than party planning, and a
  published book is a clearer product than a party.
- C2 vs a generic breakup pitch (C3/C8 shape): C2 wins on logic — proven pet
  spend beats pigeon logistics.
- C2 vs C6: both strong on logic; C2 wins on criterion 2 (dog autobiography
  is a premise nobody has heard; wedding-photo insurance sits closer to
  familiar wedding add-ons) and on punch placement.
- C2 vs C1: C2 wins — rent-a-mourner exists in the real world; a dog's
  autobiography has no real-world analogue.
- C2 vs C5: C2 wins on criterion 2 — "professional apologiser" is a
  mediator with a new name; C2's premise is unheard-of.
- C2 vs C4: C4 is stranger, but C2 wins on criterion 3, which the brief
  weighs before the turn. Per the brief, the first differing criterion
  decides "unless a later one differs by much more" — C4's criterion-2 edge
  is real, but its criterion-3 gap is bigger (no plausible payer vs proven
  pet spenders). C2 wins.
